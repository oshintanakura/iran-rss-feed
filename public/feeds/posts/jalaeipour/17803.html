If we look at the past hundred years, a large part of Iran's political history can perhaps be understood as the successive efforts of governments to build this same "state capacity" and, as a result, achieve practical independence. Reza Shah, above all else, advanced the project of territorial control: a centralized army, a bureaucracy, roads, registration, taxation, and extending the authority of the central government into regions where the state's presence had previously been weak or indirect. During Mohammad Reza Shah's era, and in a different and more overt form during the Mosaddegh movement, the question of economic independence and sovereignty over resources—especially oil—moved to the center of politics. The Islamic Republic pursued a different project: popularizing politics and mobilizing society, while simultaneously building a military and deterrent capacity unlike the classic Reza Shah-era army model. The recent war with America may be the clearest test of this transformation. Iran today, regardless of any judgment about its policies and the heavy costs of the war, is no longer the state whose official delegation needed the British to repair its car just to get through Qazvin. In today's war, against a power with overwhelming air, naval, intelligence, and economic superiority, it was able to keep fighting for months, respond to American bases with missiles and drones, and use geography, missiles, drones, regional networks, and the Strait of Hormuz to impose costs on America and its allies. The concept of independence must be sought not only in slogans and legal sovereignty, but in "the ability to resist the will of a greater power." Over the span of a hundred years, Iran's territory and core problem have not changed much: building a state that can control its territory, take command of its resources, organize its society, and—at the moment of external pressure—ensure its decisions are not independent merely on paper. The difference is that the tools of this capacity have today come to consist of a combination of social capacity, military technology, deterrence, and the power to impose costs.
