New micro-schools: mosque-schools and home-schools

In the second half of the 1990s, some households, arguing that schools were not religious enough, pulled their children out of school and created a form of home-schooling. The COVID-19 outbreak further entrenched this arrangement, albeit on a very limited scale. Out of this current, mosque-schools gradually took shape; first established in Qom, they multiplied rapidly and today have branches in 22 provinces, overseen by a central headquarters. Over the past 4 years, the number of these schools has more than doubled to over 450, with an average of roughly 30 students per school. After several years of operation, these schools were finally granted operating licenses starting in 1402 as a type of non-governmental school, and they also signed agreements with the government to expand into rural areas. Because these schools use mosque facilities, their tuition is lower, and beyond more extensive religious instruction, they also offer skills training and income-generation programs—particularly income generation through cyberspace and cryptocurrencies.

Alongside this current, a trend of establishing home-schools has also emerged, starting in Tehran and growing rapidly. These too are schools with a student body of between 20 and 40, charging very high tuition, and are often run with supplementary curricula in more private settings. Many of these home-schools are in practice smaller branches of, or affiliated with, a private school. Despite the Ministry of Education's current opposition, these schools do not conflict with the ministry's overall policy logic and face no obstacle to expansion. Home-schools are in the early stages of becoming established, and their activities have so far been reported in some areas of Tehran as well as the metropolises of Tabriz and Isfahan.

The spread of these micro-schools signals the continuation and intensification of differentiation and the ever-increasing stratification of parts of society into smaller units—a kind of intra-group and intra-class fragmentation. Mosque-schools represent a form of differentiation among more religious families themselves, just as home-schools represent further differentiation among the upper strata of society. These schools should not be conflated with diversity in the educational system, because educational diversity is positive only when it is accompanied by universal access and the possibility of genuine choice for all social groups.
