Gentlemen in charge! A society that has been economically squeezed by war, sanctions, and siege becomes more sensitive, more on edge, and more vulnerable. The smallest social conflict in such an atmosphere carries a far higher cost than under normal conditions. A family grappling with inflation, job insecurity, anxiety over medicine, housing, and their children's future has far less capacity to endure new tensions. At this point, the quality of social policy is directly tied to the country's capacity to pull through the crisis. Every policy must be measured against a simple question: Does this policy, at this moment, strengthen social bonds or further inflame existing fractures? Provocative identity debates, cultural disputes such as hijab, the proposal to lash a female singer, the widespread criminalization of social behaviors, actions that set parts of society against other parts, and the reopening of cases such as how women dress are not merely a cultural or legal matter. Under crisis conditions, these policies carry a secondary consequence as well: they divert part of society's political, disciplinary, and psychological energy away from the main problem and activate divisions that you must accept Iranian society has moved past and that the people must be respected for — yet under economic pressure, these divisions have the potential to become a social fracture. In this sense, a country in crisis needs something akin to a "social ceasefire" — meaning preventing the creation of conflicts that are not urgent and that can drain the country's limited social capital.
