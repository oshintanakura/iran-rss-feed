"He was so humble and self-effacing that he had no idea of his own greatness."

In his autobiography, the late Arthur Arberry writes of the late Reynold A. Nicholson — scholar, commentator, and translator of literary and mystical works from Persian and Arabic into English:

He was a consummate scholar, so enamored of his books that he sacrificed his eyesight to ceaseless reading, so humble and self-effacing that he had no idea of his own greatness. In the final years of his life, when the lamp of his eyes was flickering toward extinction, he composed this verse — a poem that, to me, among all the last words ever uttered by human beings, carries the truest inspiration and the most moving farewell:

When Truth appears unveiled before your eyes,
These tongues are left bewildered and surprised.
*I shall fall silent — the Beloved has come to speak:*
*Listen, and God knows best what is right and true.*
*Become devoid of sense, of hearing, and of thought,*
*That you may hear the summons "Return!"*
As long as this tongue remains, you shall not hear the Word.
So words must be brief. And peace.

**I have borrowed the Persian rendering of Nicholson's poem from verses of the Masnavi. The asterisked lines are from the Masnavi.

- Nicholson's poem, as narrated by Arberry, reads:
When Truth shines out words fail and nothing tell;
Now hear the Voice within your hearts. Farewell.

This is the closing verse of a long poem that Arberry quoted and confirmed earlier in this same piece, noting that in these verses Nicholson was deeply influenced by Rumi: ... He seldom spoke of his personal beliefs, and I, too, in twenty-five years of close friendship with him, learned little about how he was raised or of his spiritual journey. Yet the image that took shape in my mind was that he, like many others, had lost his faith in youth and then recovered it through intellectual companionship and kinship of spirit with Muslim mystics.

In his old age, he composed a poem in which, for the first time, he laid bare his innermost thoughts. It was clear that these thoughts had been profoundly shaped by long years of studying Rumi's works ...
