The next war doesn't necessarily begin with the firing of the first missile. Before that, a battle is already underway — over truth, over assigning blame, over legitimizing military action. Who broke the agreement? Who started the conflict? Who will bear responsibility for the consequences of the next war? The answers to these questions may be constructed and locked in on the media battlefield before the military conflict even begins.

Take the narrative that has taken shape in recent days around attacks on oil tankers and commercial ships: that Iran deliberately violated the Islamabad Understanding and is therefore responsible for the collapse of the agreement and the possible escalation of war. That the attacks occurred is one thing; turning that into a definitive narrative of Iran's unilateral responsibility — without examining the obligations of the parties, the US naval blockade, and the sequence of military actions — is another. Sometimes, to build a misleading narrative, there's no need to fabricate reality at all; it's enough to select part of the truth and omit the rest.

We have seen this before, too, in attempts to discredit real images of the bombing of Tehran. Some media accounts claimed the images of destruction were staged and that the women seen in the photos were actresses who kept reappearing. An independent review of the images from Sabounchi Street in Tehran refuted that claim. Here, the issue wasn't merely the publication of a false news item; it was an attempt to make the audience doubt reality itself, even when confronted with documented images of destruction and war victims.

In the possible next stage of the war, we must be more vigilant than ever against three phenomena: Misinformation — false information spread unknowingly; Disinformation — fake and distorted information deliberately produced to deceive; and Malinformation — real information used to cause harm, for example by revealing the identity or location of individuals at risk. These three can be deployed simultaneously to sow panic, destroy public trust, deny the realities of war, and prepare public opinion to accept a new conflict.

In such conditions, the war propaganda headquarters, if it exists, must monitor the information space moment by moment and respond with speed, accuracy, and verifiable evidence. Of course, countering information warfare doesn't mean constructing counter-narratives or denying unpleasant realities. The main capital in this battle is public trust, and that capital is preserved only through honesty and transparency.

The war of narratives may, before the military war, produce one crucial outcome: whom the public considers the initiator of the war, whom it recognizes as the victim, and whom it holds responsible for the destruction that follows.
