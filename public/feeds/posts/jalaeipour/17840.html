The book "In Defense of Universal Basic Services" rests on three theoretical pillars: human needs, the capability approach, and foundational economics and provisioning systems. The authors argue that compared to Universal Basic Income (UBI), the idea of Universal Basic Services (UBS)—by rebuilding social infrastructure and collective consumption while also enhancing efficiency and ecological sustainability—strengthens equality and solidarity as well, and is politically feasible because it is incremental and adaptable in nature. The authors see this idea as a path toward reclaiming the collective ideal that has been weakened under neoliberal pressures. They regard health, education, housing, transportation, care for children and the elderly, and access to digital information as necessities whose provision must be guaranteed for all through user participation and co-production in identifying needs and designing services, and through a combination of organizations—with Universal Basic Income serving merely as a supplement to cover non-service costs and to increase households' bargaining power, because if the provisioning pillar of Universal Basic Services is weak, basic income leads to the commodification and minimization of basic services.
