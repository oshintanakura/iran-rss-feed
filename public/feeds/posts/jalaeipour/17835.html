But you could see that the sustained presence of part of society in the public space was effectively producing a different image of the street: the street was not entirely in the hands of a single force. And it is precisely at this point that internal simplification can also ruin the analysis. If, because of the radical slogans of the third group, we treat the entire gathering as a factional movement, we have conflated the first body with the third group. If, because of the presence of needy people next to the mokebs, we assume the whole crowd was "brought in with food," we have substituted a social situation for a political explanation. And in the opposite direction, if, because of the presence of families and their genuine emotions, we deny any formal organizing or factional activity on the margins of the gatherings, we have once again erased part of the scene. Perhaps the most important difference between a television report and field observation is this. The camera passes over the crowd and turns a thousand people into one "people." The politician speaks from behind a desk about those same thousand people and turns them into one "current." Social media clips a few seconds of the most radical slogan and turns a thousand people into those same few who shouted loudest. But when you walk among them yourself, this unity quickly comes apart. One has come to recite prayers. One has come to say that in wartime you don't stay home. One has come because it's night and the square is crowded. One stands next to the mokeb because life has become expensive. And one is waiting for the ceremony to end so he can just begin his own political work. They are all in one street; but they are not necessarily in one gathering.3
