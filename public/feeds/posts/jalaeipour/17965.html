🔸 Postcolonial studies centered on data and cyberspace help us understand that emerging technologies do not take shape in a social and political vacuum. Data, algorithms, and artificial intelligence, like any other technology, are constructed within power relations. The question is not "What technologies do we have?" but rather "Who designs them, what interests are involved in their deployment, and which groups benefit from their advantages and disadvantages?"

🔸 The authors of this book remind us that colonialism should not be defined merely in its historical form, including the occupation of territory and the creation of colonies. Colonialism has taken different forms throughout history and continues today in a new guise, including control over data extraction infrastructures and technological dependency. In the digital age, human data, social behaviors, and collective information have become resources that are collected from around the world and processed, accumulated, and made the basis for decision-making in a few centers.

🔸 The concept of "data colonialism" introduced by the authors of the book emphasizes precisely this aspect. The issue is not that data is inherently negative or that digital technology should be abandoned; the issue is that in the current global order, ownership and control of data are severely unbalanced and are mainly in the hands of large tech giants, who use it for profit, predicting collective behaviors, and even political influence.

🔸 In addition to theoretical analyses and case studies, the authors also propose solutions for countering data colonialism in the present era.
