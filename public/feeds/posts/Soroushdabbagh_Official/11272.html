Excerpts from my recent interview with the IBNA book news agency: "If Sohrab were alive today, in the third millennium and in today's Tehran, and were present at his exhibitions, which part of what he had to say would be more worth hearing, and what would your first question to him be?" In my view, Sohrab's calmness, composure, and Stoic way of being would take on great significance; a subject about which much could be said separately. This calmness manifests in his lovemaking with and proximity to nature and a kind of "ataraxia," meaning inner tranquility, which the Stoic philosophers spoke about. Sohrab had internalized this calmness and also looked upon the world with a poetic gaze. The critique of technology, which I spoke about earlier, death-awareness and the effort to give meaning to life are also among the hearable and contemporary parts of his thought. Loving nature, paying attention to the other, and reflecting on categories such as death, love, solitude, and suffering can, in my view, still be worth hearing today. Regarding the exhibition of his paintings that you mentioned, naturally the paintings themselves matter; but I think Sohrab's words about the world and his way of living are also very important today. The reason is that in his case, there was considerable overlap between his public and private life, between his lived life and his work. He lived the way he wrote, and especially the way he composed poetry. This overlap and what we call "authenticity" can make a person charismatic and influence others; an unpretentious and intimate influence, such that one could benefit from his company and not feel the passage of time. In such conditions, the person's own presence and way of living become as important as his words. If I want to answer your question, "If you saw Sohrab, what would your first question to him be?" The truth is, I might not have had any specific question at all. For more than thirty years I have been living in Sohrab's atmosphere. Perhaps I would have liked to watch him; or, to put it better, to spend time in his company from noon until night, to chat together and experience that composure and calmness that was in his being, in socializing with him. I wanted to benefit, to the extent of my capacity, from that calmness that I believe had descended upon him. Sohrab himself said: "We ourselves are the awning of our calmness." I would have liked to catch and experience a breeze of that composure and calmness from his company...
