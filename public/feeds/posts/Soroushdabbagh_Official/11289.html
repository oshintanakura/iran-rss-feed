The murmurings of 14. Sohrab in the notebook "The Traveler" and "The Green Volume" was murmuring to himself: "The lover is always alone/ and the lover's hand is in the trembling hand of seconds/...He and the seconds sleep on light/ and he and the seconds give the best book in the world/ to the water/ and they know well/ that no fish ever/ untied the thousand and one knots of the river/ and at midnights with the old boat of illumination/ they set out on the waters of guidance/ and drive on until the manifestation of wonder." And: "Someone came and took my books/ drew a ceiling of the harmony of flowers over my head/ made my evening vast with repeated windows/ placed my table under the spirituality of rain." The contemplative Shirazi mystic also whispered to himself: "Seize the moment and drink in the rose garden, for the rose will not be there another week. Tear up the pages if you are my fellow student, for the knowledge of love is not in the notebook." And: "Speak of the minstrel and wine and seek less the secret of time, for no one has unraveled and no one will unravel this riddle through philosophy." And: "What is this tall ceiling, simple yet of many patterns? Of this riddle, no sage in the world is aware." What a strange creature the human being is! A two-legged migratory bird who passes night and day in this existence, estranged, in toil and turmoil. On one hand, he knows that acquiring knowledge and understanding is, in Aristotle's words, among the "virtues" that constitute the essence of your and my humanity; a knowledge found in books and notebooks that draws the wise in its wake. On the other hand, he sees wayfarers like Hafez and Sepehri, who were learned and erudite, at a stage of their existential journey laying down their shields, acknowledging the enigmatic and mysterious nature of existence, relinquishing the untying of knots, washing away the pages of lessons and exercises and notebooks and books and entrusting them to flowing water, placing "the burden of knowledge from the shoulders of the swallow onto the ground"; and they have tasted and savored a "transparent fear," a "wise silence," and a strange wonder and bewilderment that consumes the palate.

Quoted from the book: "Velvety Murmurings," Volume Two.
