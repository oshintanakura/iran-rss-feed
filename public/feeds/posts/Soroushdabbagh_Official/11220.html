The whisper of 12. The autumn season has arrived; an autumn that breathes with the blowing of its winds and the turning of its trees' leaves from green to yellow. The month of Mehr is bound to the name and memory of three luminous stars in the sky of Iranian culture and art; cherished souls whose long and deep companionship over the past three decades has polished my soul and gladdened my days: Hafez Shirazi, Sohrab Sepehri, and Mohammadreza Shajarian. Shajarian and Sepehri were born in the month of Mehr. The anniversary commemorating the thinking recluse of Shiraz also falls in this month. I am glad that the lecture series "In the Presence of Hafez" begins the day after tomorrow, in the month of Mehr. In this lecture series, I intend to offer my own re-reading and re-creation of thirty ghazals from the Divan of Hafez and to narrate my roughly forty years of familiarity and intimacy with Hafez's life-world here and now. The opening verse of the first ghazal I have chosen for re-reading and narrative-making is as follows: "If the Sufi drinks wine in measure, may it be sweet to him / Otherwise, may he forget the thought of this affair." Other exquisite verses are also found in this ghazal: "Our elder said no error befell the pen of creation / Bravo to his pure vision that covers errors!" and "The King of Turks listens to the words of claimants / May he be ashamed of the injustice done to the sweet Siyavash!" I have repeatedly read the verses of this stunning artistic work of Hafez over the past decades and have gazed upon them and into them. In this process and in the long future ahead, it is as though both I was reading the ghazal and the ghazal was reading me. For this reason, my present understanding of "pure vision that covers errors" and "shame from the injustice of Siyavash's blood" has noticeably changed. This epistemic transformation can also be explained in the language of Gadamerian hermeneutics and by borrowing the doctrine of the "fusion of horizons." It is obvious that Khwajeh Shams al-Din Mohammad Hafez composed his ghazals seven hundred years ago and read his story into the ear of history. You and I, who are today the audience of these poems, belong to a different context, time, and historical horizon. But this does not prevent the important matter that we strive to the best of our ability, in the light of our lived experience here and now, to become the "audience" of these poems and to understand them; an address that takes place in today's horizon and is the product of our creative and methodical encounter with the text. Of course, in this kind of dialectical encounter with the text, the beliefs, values, attitudes, and the "soul" and "world" of you and me, which have changed over time, pour forth, play an important role in the "process" of addressing the text, and create novel "products" and place them before others."
