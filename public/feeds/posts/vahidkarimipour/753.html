Consequences of geopolitical stubbornness. On August 2, 1990, the Iraqi army, which at that time was called the fourth army in the world with 56 divisions and 700 fighter jets, invaded Kuwait in a lightning attack and declared it its nineteenth province. The international community, despite scattered objections from some countries like Jordan, Yemen, and the Palestine Liberation Organization, took a position against Saddam with a decisive majority. Just a few days after the invasion, the Security Council imposed the heaviest economic sanctions in history up to that time against Iraq with Resolution 661. Then the Security Council, through Resolution 678, gave Iraq until January 15, 1991, to withdraw unconditionally from Kuwait. In this interval, numerous peace plans and many mediations were offered to Iraq by the Soviet Union, France, Arab countries, and the UN Secretary-General; probably about 27 times. But Saddam, in "geopolitical stubbornness," made the withdrawal from Kuwait conditional on: Israel's withdrawal from occupied Palestinian territories and the liberation of holy Jerusalem. Saddam thought his capable army could inflict heavy casualties on American forces and that American public opinion would not tolerate a long, attritional war like Vietnam. He also estimated that the international and Arab coalition would fall apart with the start of the war. During the 90-day period of autumn 1990, all peace plans and mediations were rejected one after another by Saddam. The belief in the war becoming attritional, inflicting heavy casualties on America and defeating its public opinion, and the incorrect prediction about the collapse of the international coalition were completely false propositions on which Saddam's strategy of resistance was based. With the rejection of the last peace proposals, in January 1991, the operation called Desert Storm began. After a 39-day air war, during which Iraq's power grid, refineries, bridges, command centers, and communication routes were destroyed or rendered unusable, in a 100-hour ground battle, the defensive lines of the Iraqi army were worn down, and this army, with tens of thousands of casualties and the destruction of equipment, was forced to leave Kuwait. The heavy bombing of the Basra highway turned it into the "Highway of Death." Finally, Saddam's commanders at the Safwan tent, accepting the heavy ceasefire conditions of the coalition and America, surrendered with humiliating terms: -Destruction of missile and chemical weapons; -Acceptance of unconditional entry of UN and IAEA inspectors anywhere in Iraq; -And revision of borders in favor of Kuwait.
