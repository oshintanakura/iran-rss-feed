On the Moscow train, the St. Petersburg to Moscow route was high-speed. Of course, its speed never exceeded 199 kilometers per hour. It covered the roughly 800-kilometer distance between the two cities in exactly 4 hours. The route was entirely flat and forested, covered with young trees. Occasionally, a farm would appear in the middle of the forests. The train passed by several small towns and stopped for only one minute at just two or three intermediate stations. The lush green steppes on both sides of the railway reminded me of a scene from Andrei Tarkovsky's film Mirror—the scene where a beautiful, sorrowful woman sits on the trunk of a dry, fallen tree, waiting for her husband to return from the war front. In that scene, when the wind sweeps through the meadow, it seems as if a glimpse of paradise appears on earth. As it happened, the male voice that informed passengers of the status of intermediate stations sounded like Tarkovsky's voice. The man's voice resonated in my ears like the recitation of a poem, as delicate as the murmur of a gentle autumn rain. I said to myself, how could such a seemingly rough alphabet produce words so fluent, delicate, and sweet? Moscow's air temperature was a few degrees higher than St. Petersburg's, but fortunately, it remained at a mild and pleasant level. Upon exiting the old railway station, Stalin's architectural legacy became evident before our eyes. The massive government buildings, known as the Seven Sisters, dominated central Moscow. They were built by Stalin's order to give grandeur to Moscow. From the very first moment of arriving in the capital, the impatience of its residents compared to those of St. Petersburg was apparent! This impatience manifested itself in the ear-splitting honks of some nervous and hasty drivers in the midday traffic.
