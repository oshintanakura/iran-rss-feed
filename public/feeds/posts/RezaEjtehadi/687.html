🔹 When ignorant fools are seated at the table In a series of behavioral experiments and animal intelligence tests, an animal—be it a rat, dolphin, or bird—is kept in a cage and must perform a task desired by the experimenter to receive a reward. For example, it must press a lever to open a hatch and receive some food, and repeat this for more food. Intelligent animals eventually learn how to get food. A more complex form of these experiments is when the lever and the food source are far apart. The animal must perform an action on one side of the cage and receive the reward on the other. Obviously, discovering this secret requires more intelligence. In a series of experiments first conducted with pigs in the late 1970s*, later known in game theory as the "Pig Game" (Boxed Pigs), two pigs are placed in a cage simultaneously. Now suppose that in this game's design, the distance between the lever and the food is very large, and also suppose that the two pigs differ in intelligence. The smarter pig eventually figures out how to get the food and presses the lever. But by the time it reaches the food source, a large portion has been eaten by the stupid pig, leaving little for it. There is no hope for the stupid pig, so it goes all the way again to press the lever, and the same story repeats. The stupid pig doesn't even need to think about where the food comes from. It sits there waiting for a hand from the unseen to pour some food. The continuation of this story turns the smart pig into a slave, constantly striving and moving from one side of the cage to the other to provide food, and the stupid pig into a pampered, lazy master. https://t.me/RezaEjtehadi ------------------ * Baldwin and Meese (1979)
