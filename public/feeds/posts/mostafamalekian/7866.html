🔴 Hate the mistake, not the mistaken
✍️ Mostafa Malekian

One of the important points in ethics is distinguishing the mistake from the mistaken. You can hate a bad deed, fight against it, and even prevent it from continuing—without hating the human being who committed that mistake.

Why?

• Hating the mistaken targets the person; whereas love for the human being is a prerequisite for reform. If we consider the person our enemy, then reforming them will no longer be our main goal.

• It helps us see our own mistakes too. When we focus on "finding faults" instead of "finding fault-doers," we realize that we ourselves might commit the same mistakes under certain circumstances.

• Emotions do not override rationality. Hatred of a person can impair our judgment and cause us to seek merely punishment or elimination of the individual, rather than correcting a behavior.

• We do not rush to eliminate the mistaken. The goal is not to eliminate the person; it is to stop the mistake and provide the possibility of reform.

This perspective does not mean being passive in the face of mistakes or injustice. You can firmly fight against a wrong behavior, even stop the mistaken person, but your motivation should not be personal revenge or hatred.

Gandhi has a saying that well illustrates this distinction:
"I hate the wrong deed, but I love human beings and do not hate those who do wrong."

Perhaps it is more ethical to say:
Fight the mistake, not the person.

Because if our goal is to reform the world, we cannot exclude human beings from the circle of our love and compassion—even when we must stand against their mistakes.
