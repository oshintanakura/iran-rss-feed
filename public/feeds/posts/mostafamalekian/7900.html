🔴I Have Not Been Happy ✍️ From an interview with Mostafa Malekian. Published in Andishe Pouya 102

You have always said that spirituality is a process that brings tranquility, joy, and hope. As you pass seventy, have you yourself attained this tranquility, joy, and hope?

🔹As for tranquility, I am content and I believe I have it. As for hope, I am also content. But joy, no. From childhood onward, I think — perhaps due to my psychological makeup, or perhaps due to my childhood environment — I have fundamentally never been happy. This one thing I truly lack, and I have never truly laughed from the bottom of my heart; never.

Looking back on your life from the vantage point of seventy, what was the hardest period of your life? And what was the most precious price you paid for intellectual honesty?

🔹I think the most precious price I paid was losing friends. I am a person who cherishes friendship, and friendship matters greatly to me. To this day, I myself have never cut ties with anyone. But with every intellectual transformation I underwent, I lost a number of friends for whom I was very dear. It was as though their beliefs mattered more to them, and when I changed my beliefs, they would sever their friendship with me.

🔹The hardest period of my life was when I forsook many outward trappings and privileges in order to bring about, in my own estimation, a transformation in the Qom seminary. At that time I was also devout in every sense; out of compassion and mercy, I wanted the educational, training, and research system of the seminary to change so that the very goals they held would be realized and achieved in the best possible way. For example, I myself began changing the textbooks — that is, I was teaching, but not with the seminary textbooks, rather with books I deemed better. But gradually I saw that I was far too small to accomplish anything against this very old edifice. During those eighteen years in which I wanted to do something in Qom, I was awarded a scholarship twice to be sent abroad, and I went neither time. It always seemed to me that if this transformation took place in the seminaries, it would be more beneficial for society. But I returned unsuccessful. In the year 1372, after much pressure, I came and bought a house in Tehran and returned to Tehran to live. Again some of the gentlemen insisted that no, stay in the seminary. I gave back the very house I had bought in Tehran, and returned to Qom once more, staying there another seven years until the year 1379. But I saw that everything was falling apart.
