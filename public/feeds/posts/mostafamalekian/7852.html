🔴Shariati was above all an orator✍Mostafa Malekian🔹 Shariati was, above all, an orator. I mean he played with the feelings, emotions, and passions of his audience. But the intellectuals after the revolution are more teachers and instructors. Even in their lectures, they teach. When you listen to a lecture by Dr. Ali Shariati, you experience a surge of feelings, emotions, and passions. You say he's right, bravo, and hurrah... But when his lecture ends, if someone asks you what the essence of Dr. Shariati's lecture was, you'll find that, like a fish slipping from your grasp, you have nothing in your hand.🔹You can't say, for example, that the lecture had an introduction, three preliminary points, one thesis, and two arguments. Because he was an orator. He was from the region of Khorasan, and in that region we had masterful orators, like Fakhreddin Hejazi. "Much ado about nothing." God rest his soul, but whenever I think of him, I recall Shakespeare's saying, "Much ado about nothing." Shariati was an orator, and I mean that in the sense of Aristotle's five arts of rhetoric. That is, he dealt with emotions, feelings, and passions.🔹But when you sit and listen to the post-revolution intellectuals, your feelings, emotions, and passions are not engaged, and you constantly have to think about what his argument was. [...] When I say this is not a condemnation, I mean they are teachers. That is, they work with the audience's mind, they argue, they present theses, they critique the arguments of opponents, and they seek to strengthen their own reasons. Can you recall any lecture by Dr. Shariati where he says, "In this lecture, I have two theses? The first thesis is supported by one argument, and the second by three arguments." In all of Shariati's lectures, you cannot find a single instance like that.➖Part of a conversation between Ali Zamaniyan and Mostafa Malekian@mostafamalekian
