"This is Iran." Now we can return to the famous narrative. Critics of de-escalation are right on one point: cooperation in Afghanistan did not force America to change its grand strategy toward Iran. One cannot conclude from this that any limited cooperation with Washington necessarily leads to improved relations. But one also cannot conclude from it that the principle of cooperating against the Taliban was wrong. The Taliban was a threat to Iran, and its fall benefited Iran. In the 2013 debate, Hassan Rouhani's short answer to Jalili showed where the real disagreement lay: "But the country's national interests were preserved." This answer does not exonerate the Khatami government of all its errors; it merely sets a different criterion: a foreign policy decision should be judged by Iran's interest at the time it was made, not by the degree of the other side's loyalty in the future. On the other hand, Bonn should not be romanticized either. Iran-US cooperation was formed on ground full of conflict; from Palestine and Israel and Hezbollah to the missile program and the fragmentation of the decision-making structure in Tehran. And Israel? The available evidence does not allow us to say that the Karine A was designed from the outset to destroy Iran-US relations; its intelligence operation had begun months before September 11. But the evidence is strong for a narrower and more important proposition: after the ship's seizure, Israel turned this intelligence success into political capital against any rapprochement between Tehran and Washington. Ultimately, the Bush administration also had another path. It could have maintained cooperation in Afghanistan and pursued the dispute over Palestine separately; it could have used the existing channel to demand an explanation; and it could have distinguished between cooperation on one file and conflict on another. But it decided to judge Iran in its "totality" and to announce that judgment to the world with one of the most absolute possible terms, "evil." One should not conclude from history that America is trustworthy; nor is it necessary to shorten history in order to prove distrust of America. Iran's cooperation in Afghanistan was not necessarily a mistake; the Karine A alone was not the cause of the axis of evil; Israel did not design the whole affair; and America was not a helpless victim of events. The fifty-five lost days say exactly this: politics usually happens in the gap between two sentences that are later placed side by side to construct a narrative.
