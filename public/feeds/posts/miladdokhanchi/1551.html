For complex human problems, we shouldn't be looking for technical answers! If a pipe in your house bursts, that's a technical problem; but when your neighbor puts their trash out earlier than they're supposed to, that's no longer merely technical — it belongs to the realm of human problems. In teaching problem-solving, the first thing we draw the learner's attention to is distinguishing the technical problem from the human problem, and when we talk about problem-solving and teaching it, we mean the second category — because technical problems require expertise in their own field. If my phone stops working, I go to a specialist and they fix it. But human problems don't necessarily have predetermined solutions, and technical expertise alone won't help solve them. The solution to these problems has to be discovered, and solving them requires a basic understanding of the humanities and learning the skill of problem-solving. The biggest mistake is usually offering technical solutions for problems with human roots. When my employees' motivation is low and they show up late to work because of it, I can't force them into line with punishment, incentives, and a few new rules; I need to ask why their motivation has dropped, invite them into a conversation, and address the problem at the level of human relationships. Another mistake is assuming that our technical expertise can help us solve human problems — which it absolutely cannot. Yet another mistake is framing problems with human roots in technical terms. In that same example of lateness, if I say the reason is that they wake up late and that a morning phone call will get them to work on time, I've framed a human problem in technical terms!
