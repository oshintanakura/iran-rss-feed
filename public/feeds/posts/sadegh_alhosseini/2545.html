Oil transit through Hormuz rises: The White House celebrated, the market didn't buy it
Sadegh Al-Hosseini
Kelley Vlahos, editor of Responsible Statecraft, made important points in yesterday's article that I'll get into below. He says last week the White House and its supporters announced "the strait is open and we've won." On Sunday, however, Brent was still holding at $102. The gap between these two images is what this article is about.
What's really going on behind the numbers? Daily oil transit through Hormuz has reached 13 to 17 million barrels, down from about 20 million before the war. But a large portion of it is happening through an emergency workaround: state-owned ships pass through the strait with their tracking systems turned off and hand off their cargo to supertankers in safe waters. The cost of chartering a supertanker from the Persian Gulf to Asia has hit $1.2 million, insurance has gotten more expensive, and ships carrying fertilizer, aluminum, petrochemicals, and grain are still stranded in the Persian Gulf. America has sent a third aircraft carrier to the region, and its navy is, according to experts, stretched thin.
The drawdown from reserves is also heavy: 400 million barrels drawn from reserves in March, another 100 million barrels from the G7, America's strategic reserves at their lowest level since 1983, and JPMorgan saying it sees no end to this situation.
In other words, Iran can't fully close the strait, and America can't fully open it. Unless Trump reaches a deal with Iran; that's what the market is really waiting for.
Why is this news encouraging for us?
1. Trump's leverage has hit its ceiling. With three aircraft carriers, the strait has only become "half-open." When American experts themselves say the only way out is a deal with Iran, it means the military option costs more than it delivers.
2. The clock is working against the White House. The November midterm elections are approaching, and according to experts, fuel prices won't come down by then; the soaring cost of diesel has even hit American farmers. This pressure raises the incentive for a deal. But it's clear that the cost of this war is being paid by the people of Iran.
3. A deal would untie many knots at once. A world whose reserves have run dry is thirsty for fresh supply; it's not just oil — petrochemicals, fertilizer, and metals are also stuck behind this barrier.
The world has come to believe that Hormuz today is neither closed nor open; and the key is not on warships but on the negotiating table.
