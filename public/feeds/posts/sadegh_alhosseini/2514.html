The hidden shock absorber of the oil market in this war was China's reserves. Before the war, Beijing had stockpiled about 1.4 billion barrels. When Hormuz was closed, it cut its purchases to a ten-year low and drew down its reserves; that very absence neutralized part of the price shock. Now the tables have turned: August imports reached 8.93 million barrels per day, and Shanghai oil is trading at a premium above Brent. But this is not a recovery in consumption; China's gasoline and diesel consumption is still 8 and 9 percent below last year. China is buying oil to fill its reserves, and it knows this decline in consumption is not permanent either. That is, China has gone from being a price brake to a gas pedal. Until yesterday, its absence kept prices in check; today, its presence pushes them up, and the cost of prolonging the war comes out of Beijing's pocket—and that just two weeks before Xi's trip to Washington. But China's mediation has always been low-risk; it steps in when an agreement is almost ripe, and Trump is not one to commit to any lasting deal. Beijing has found an incentive to end this war, but it issues the bill in the name of Washington and Tehran.@sadegh_alhosseini
