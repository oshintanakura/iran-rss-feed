The real innovation of this plan lies in five points: The policy unit shifts from "carrier" to "service." Today, gasoline, gas, electricity, water, and transportation are largely managed in separate platforms. The plan says every decision should be measured based on the final output: "one unit of cooling," "one unit of heating," and "one unit of mobility" — with what combination of resources, technology, and cost they are provided. Four sources and three services are placed in a common decision matrix. For example, replacing gasoline with electricity is only desirable if its effect on the electricity balance, power plant gas, air, and network cost is measured simultaneously. This prevents "transferring imbalance from one carrier to another." Price reform becomes a functional contract rather than a financial measure. The price of excess consumption only rises when a real possibility of reducing consumption has been created; in mobility, that means travel options; in heating, controls and equipment; in cooling, low-consumption technology. This is an operational rule, not a slogan. Instead of buying equipment, the government buys "verified savings." Heaters, coolers, motors, buses, or boiler rooms are not the main subjects of payment. Payment is made against cubic meters of gas, kilowatt-hours of electricity, cubic meters of water, peak megawatts, or actually freed fuel. This change alters the financial and incentive model of the program. Three major consumption markets are redesigned simultaneously and complementarily. In mobility: vehicle–energy–time–cost; in heating: building–equipment–control–behavior; in cooling: electricity–water–peak–comfort. Thus, instead of dozens of scattered projects, three national "savings factories" are formed.
