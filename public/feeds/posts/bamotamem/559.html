B&R Program - Part Three

My take is that you can learn as much about China's ideas, strategies, and orientations from B&R as you cannot from BRICS and the Shanghai Cooperation Organization.

I know it's wrong to compare a program with an organization, a forum, or a treaty. But I'm making this point in the sense that in B&R, China's hands are untied and it isn't bogged down in coordinating with multiple governments. On the other hand, it has to directly allocate resources to projects. So maybe in BRICS and the SCO, there are places where they exchange pleasantries and engage in what you might call political flirting, but in B&R there's no room for those games anymore. They really have to lay out their decisions and policies clearly and transparently, and actually implement them.

Although it's never been officially stated outright, in my view (personal interpretation), one of the main reasons China at one point pushed Iran and Saudi Arabia to negotiate and clear the air between them probably goes back to B&R. If we set aside the things that get written on paper, signed, and photographed, in practice China has had the largest volume of B&R cooperation in our region with Saudi Arabia, the UAE, and Iraq. Cooperation with Iran hasn't been small either. But sanctions, banking restrictions, and other considerations have made the scale of cooperation different.

The horizon China has for B&R spans several decades. And naturally, it aligns the decisions, policies, and resources it allocates with this project as much as possible.

In a way, you could call it a super-framework for economic and investment affairs.

By the way, Pakistan is also one of China's close partners in this program. Of course, beyond all this, Pakistan has a very deep relationship with China. In the media, it's usually referred to as an ironclad relationship (China doesn't throw around such terms and expressions lightly).

The last point worth paying attention to is that China insists strongly on defining its interactions — whether in B&R or other cooperations — as a partnership, and emphasizes that it is not one for alliance. Its logic is that partnership makes sense between two parties. But alliance is usually formed against a third country, or assumes (or creates) a third rival.

This strategy leads China in many places not to behave the way its partners expect of it.
