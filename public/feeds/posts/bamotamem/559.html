The B&R Program – Part Three

My take is that you can learn as much about China's ideas, strategies, and orientations from B&R as you can from BRICS and the Shanghai Cooperation Organization.

I know that comparing a program to an organization, a forum, or a treaty is flawed. But I'm making this point in the sense that within B&R, China's hands are free and it isn't bogged down in coordinating with multiple governments. At the same time, it has to allocate resources directly to projects. So maybe in BRICS and the SCO, there are moments of pleasantries and what you might call political flirting, but in B&R there's no room for those games. They have to genuinely lay out their decisions and policies clearly and transparently, and actually implement them.

Although it has never been officially stated outright, in my view (a personal interpretation), one of the main reasons China at one point encouraged Iran and Saudi Arabia to negotiate and clear up their grievances probably comes back to B&R. Setting aside the things that get written on paper, signed, and photographed (Iranian officials have racked up plenty of those photos with China), in practice China's largest volume of B&R cooperation in our region has been with Saudi Arabia, the UAE, and Iraq. Cooperation with Iran hasn't been insignificant either. But sanctions, banking restrictions, and other considerations have made the scale of cooperation different.

The horizon China has for B&R spans several decades. And naturally, it aligns the decisions, policies, and resources it allocates with this project as much as possible.

In a way, you could call it a super-framework for economic and investment affairs.

By the way, Pakistan is also one of China's close partners in this program. Of course, beyond all that, Pakistan has a very deep relationship with China. In the media, it's usually referred to as an ironclad relationship (China doesn't throw around such terms and expressions lightly).

The last point worth paying attention to is that China insists strongly on defining its interactions — whether in B&R or in other cooperations — as a partnership, and emphasizes that it is not the type for alliance. The logic is that a partnership makes sense between two parties. But an alliance is usually formed against a third country, or assumes (or creates) a third rival.

This strategy leads China, in many instances, not to behave the way its partners expect it to.
