National Wealth Fund

This Oxford Handbook on sovereign wealth funds has gotten a bit dated (2017). Especially in the financial and investment industry, which has seen rapid changes. But its structure, the framework it offers, and the points it makes about the architecture of sovereign wealth funds are truly excellent.

You probably know that one of the most famous funds belongs to Norway, where the government saves and invests oil revenues through a mechanism. They call it an intergenerational perspective. Their government says the state has no right to spend oil money recklessly, because it also belongs to future generations.

They started thirty years ago. Now it's around 2,400 billion dollars. That's roughly half a million dollars per Norwegian.

Interestingly, nearly 70 percent of this money is the returns generated by the oil money itself that have been added to the fund. In one chapter of this book, it examines this fund's policies, which was interesting. One of them is that they never take more than a 10% stake (with voting rights) in any company, so they don't have to get involved in the day-to-day affairs of companies. It should just be an investment and they take their money.

From this perspective, they differ from approaches like Saudi Arabia's (PIF), which uses national money to buy large stakes in companies.
