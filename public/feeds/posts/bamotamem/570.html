My grandmother and some AI experts and enthusiasts have something in common

One of the most delightful letters of recent years is one that a number of Fields Medal winners (the same medal through which we know Maryam Mirzakhani) published publicly, without a specific addressee:

A Severe Misalignment of AI in Mathematics

This letter was published at a time when people — most of whom, as it happens, were not experts in mathematics — were excitedly talking in the media and on social networks about AI's ability to solve unsolved mathematical problems (some Erdős problems and a proposed proof for the existence and smoothness of Navier-Stokes).

This excitement is understandable. Understanding mathematics is hard for many of us. Unlike intelligence (which fortunately none of us complain about the distribution of, and we believe we've received a larger share than others), mathematical talent is something we readily admit to lacking.

So it's only natural that a machine being able to solve unsolved problems is astonishing.

Every sentence of the mathematicians' letter is worth reading. It can't be summarized in a Telegram post (we'll review it more carefully later on Motamem).

But if I want to summarize the text in my own words, their point is that in many mathematical problems, more important than "finding the answer" are the solutions, ideas, and methods found along the way, which draw a bigger and clearer picture of the land of mathematics.

Unsolved problems are something like rare veins of gold in a mine. Thousands of problems must be posed and solved so that, among them, a beautiful "unsolved" problem remains — one the new generation of mathematicians can test their strength against and use to discover new territories in the land of mathematics.

A problem that is solved by machine destroys one of these potential opportunities and damages a mine whose resources are not easily replenished.

I remember when we were kids, my grandmother, who apparently had grown tired of the loud sound of the TV broadcasting a football match, muttered in protest: "They should get ten or twenty balls and give them to these young guys so they don't all have to run after one ball like this."

These days, seeing each and every mathematical problem that "burns and is lost" by being solved, and the public joy (outside the community of mathematicians), I remember my grandmother's words. I feel that some people are happy that hundreds of balls have been thrown into the middle of the mathematics football field.
