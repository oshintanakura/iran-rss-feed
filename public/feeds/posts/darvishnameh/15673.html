🔶 Mohammad Darvish: Desalinating Oman Sea water is choosing between bad and worse

The government of Masoud Pezeshkian inaugurated the first phase of the project to transfer water from the Oman Sea to the Central Plateau on the 15th of Azar — a plan that Isfahan's industries see as a lifesaver, but some experts warn about its environmental consequences, its energy intensity, and the threat it poses to the Makran coast.

In this context, Mohammad Darvish, an environmental activist, stressed in an interview with Deutsche Welle Persian that "implementing the desalination project for the Oman Sea and transferring water to Isfahan could have far fewer adverse environmental and security consequences than implementing projects to transfer water from the headwaters of the Karun and Dez to Isfahan."

He added: "Unfortunately, the insistence on carrying out projects such as Cheshmeh Langan and Khodangestan and drying up two headwaters of the Dez River, pursuing destructive projects such as the Mandegan Dam and diverting the Marbor River from the headwaters of the Karun, the Khersan 3 Dam and the Beheshtabad project, the plan to transfer water from the Zayandeh Rud to Yazd, and drawing water from the Zayandeh Rud to develop certain orchards have dealt the biggest blow to the resilience of the Dez, Karun, and Zayandeh Rud watersheds and have fueled widespread discontent in the region."

Darvish concludes that "desalinating sea water itself has worrisome environmental consequences, but when choosing between bad and worse, it is preferable."

At the same time, this environmental activist warns that "this water should not be spent on further industrial development and implementing more projects in Isfahan," and adds: "Additional pressure on the provinces of Isfahan, Yazd, and Markazi in the form of polluting and water-intensive industries must be stopped."

Many independent experts point out that Iran's water crisis will not be solved by transferring new resources. If consumption management is not reformed, if water-intensive agriculture does not change its practices, and if water-intensive industries are not relocated from arid regions, even the largest water transfer projects will only buy time and deepen the current crisis.
