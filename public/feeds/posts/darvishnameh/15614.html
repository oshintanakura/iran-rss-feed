📳 Baspar Exclusive/ When ships sit idle, what happens beneath the water's surface? Why has the prolonged stoppage of ships in the Persian Gulf once again brought the issue of marine coatings to the fore?🔸 Baspar/Iran Polymer The maritime crisis in the Persian Gulf and restricted traffic along the route leading to the Strait of Hormuz is not merely a matter of scheduling, loading, or cargo delivery. For a ship that remains stationary in the water for weeks or months, some of the most significant changes take place where they cannot be seen from the deck: below the water's surface. The hull surface, in constant contact with seawater — under conditions where the ship has been deprived of its normal movement — enters a biological and hydrodynamic process that can, once the ship returns to its route, manifest as reduced speed, increased engine load, higher fuel consumption, and diminished voyage efficiency.✍️ Golrokh Fard Zolfaghari, PhD student in Polymer Engineering 🔸 A field example of this situation has also been observed with one of the Hapag-Lloyd ships. After six to eight weeks in water at a temperature of about 30 degrees Celsius, this ship encountered a significant amount of biofouling. Biofouling and barnacles had covered the entire surface of the propeller and most of the ship's vertical surfaces, and about 40 percent of the underwater hull surface was also covered with this growth. The company's CEO stated that the amount of biofouling that had developed was such that the ship was forced to move at a lower speed when passing through the Strait of Hormuz.🔸 According to SAFETY4SEA, increased biofouling can lead to reduced ship speed, higher fuel consumption, increased engine load, and greater emissions of pollutants.
