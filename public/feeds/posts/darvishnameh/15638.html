In sum, in recent years we have not treated our water resources properly and have created many problems in our country's environmental and territorial foundations, the most important of which can be summarized as: the drying up of the country's important lakes and wetlands such as Hor al-Azim, Shadegan, Gavkhouni, Jazmurian, Hamoun, and Lake Urmia; the severe drop in groundwater levels across the country's important plains and land subsidence; the growth of dust centers; the problem of aquatic and terrestrial life and pollution in the downstream stretches of rivers; and seawater intrusion into rivers. Therefore, now that there is a possibility of a large volume of water falling from the sky, we can welcome it with two approaches. One approach, rooted in fear and threat, is to focus all our efforts on safely channeling the huge volume of water to the sea, which may also alleviate a few of the above-mentioned problems along its path. But the other approach focuses on making the utmost beneficial use of this yield so that we can solve many of the above-mentioned problems beforehand. Pursuing the second approach, which is certainly in the interest of our country's present and future, requires precise planning, structural works, and, more broadly, a change in how we view this phenomenon. Planning for flood spreading, maximum recharge of aquifers in the country's critical plains, the planned channeling of floods toward wetlands, channeling floods toward dust centers, and finally the safe channeling of floods toward downstream areas and the sea — all of this can be seen as the result of shifting our view of El Niño from a curse to a blessing, and can turn our fear of facing it into an opportunity. And in the end, we can ask ourselves: if the predictions come true, will we be good hosts to welcome El Niño's water yield?
