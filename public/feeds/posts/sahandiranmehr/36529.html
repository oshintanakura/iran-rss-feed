✔️ A dream that returned to die
Written by: Sahand Iranmehr

Eurydice had died once. Orpheus was allowed to bring her back from the underworld; but right at the threshold of freedom, he looked back and lost her forever. Sometimes history's plan for those whose fate is tied to an inevitable death is exactly this: it gives a dead thing a chance to return—not to revive it, but so that its death becomes final this time.

Perhaps "57" was not an absolute end for the monarchy—or whatever other name was later given to it; it was a historical wound whose final fate could still be doubted. But if today some supporters of that dream have pinned their hopes on Israel's attack and its plans for Iran—from partition to nuclear threats, with Trump as a hired hand—to bring it back, and to realize this dream they are forced to stand ever more openly, recklessly, and violently alongside this project, they may, just like Orpheus, realize at the very moment they think they have found a chance at life again that they themselves have sealed the absolute death of the idea of monarchy and kingship in the minds of Iranians.

Then "57" will no longer be just the date of the monarchy's fall; it could become the moment when the idea of monarchy, after a second chance, lost the possibility of return forever.

Orpheus lost Eurydice once more because he looked back right at the brink of her revival. Perhaps some political dreams are like that too: they fail once, but they get a second chance not to return, but to die for good.

Sometimes an idea does not die from its first crushing defeat and blow, but from the way its followers choose to resurrect it.
