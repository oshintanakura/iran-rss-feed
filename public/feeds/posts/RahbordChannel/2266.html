If the Arabs do not price oil in dollars, should we say goodbye to dollar hegemony? You have probably heard a lot that the dollar's superior position in the world has one clear reason: America's historical collusion with the oil-rich Arab states of the Persian Gulf to price oil in dollars (especially after the oil shock of the seventies and even before that). So if the Arabs give up pricing in dollars and, for example, turn to the yuan, dollar hegemony will also go away! But in an interesting Financial Times article, it is said that this is not the case. The percentage of countries' dollar foreign exchange reserves alone does not tell us much. Basically, the current position of the dollar depends more on a more important factor than the power of the Yankee government: the Eurodollar system! But what does Eurodollar mean? It means dollars outside the borders of America! Dollars over which the Federal Reserve (the central bank) does not have serious control. In fact, about 40% of the world's dollars, i.e., about 14 trillion dollars, are not inside America, and banks around the world (London, Tokyo, or even Dubai) themselves create dollars in their accounting books and lend to customers and settle; for final settlement, of course, America comes into play, but this system should be considered more "non-governmental" and "decentralized." The irony of history is that the initial motivation for creating the Eurodollar system was brought about by the Soviet government. Also, precedent and habit are part of the story; the dollar is the common language of the business community. Merchants around the world prefer to denominate their contracts in dollars for convenience and risk management. This is what is also called the network effect, meaning when others act in a certain way, you also follow the rest for your own convenience. Moreover, Yankee treasury bonds are considered the safest assets in the world, and there are trillions of dollars of debt worldwide that must be repaid in dollars, so demand for this currency always exists; even if oil sales in dollars stop. To summarize, contrary to popular belief, dollar hegemony will not collapse with a change in the unit of oil pricing (e.g., China's yuan). The dollar is the operating system of the world's private banking and has an extensive network that is very difficult, if not impossible, to replace.
