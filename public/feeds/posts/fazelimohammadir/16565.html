One of the important yet under-examined questions in Iran's contemporary history is why Iran, in the age of colonialism, despite its economic and military weakness and its proximity to two great imperial powers, managed to preserve its political independence and avoid becoming a formal colony. In the nineteenth century, before the discovery of oil, Iran—unlike India—did not possess vast, contiguous agricultural lands with a high capacity for producing commercial crops. Unlike China, it had no consumer market with a population in the hundreds of millions, and unlike certain other territories, known gold and silver mines were not the primary source of its economic appeal to colonial powers. Nor was Iran part of the Ottoman Empire, such that it would have been caught up in the partition of Ottoman territories among the European powers. Nevertheless, Iran held extraordinary geopolitical importance. Its geographic position—between Tsarist Russia and British-ruled India—had made the country one of the most important arenas of imperial rivalry. Under such circumstances, Iran's survival cannot be attributed merely to chance or to a lack of resources attractive to colonialists.
